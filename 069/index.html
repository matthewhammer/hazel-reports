Gnome World’s Water and Way, in Hazel

^report(68) found the water to be 88% to 93% of the speed run’s steps, and the run’s search for its way to be able to make a long step. Matthew asked for both as next steps: to make the water cheaper, so the run goes faster at the same frame rate, with a Hazel version of the water, off by default, to turn on to profile and measure; and to find the run’s way by A*, in Hazel. This report gives the outcome. Both are now written in Hazel, each giving the same answers as the page’s JavaScript to the bit, and both are off by default, because Hazel takes many times as long. The JavaScript got cheaper too.

Outline#

  1. The builds: 0.0.149, and 0.0.150 with its three options.
  2. Where the water’s time goes: real flow, not idle blocks; but in 310 of about 338 blocks a step, the most that moves is under a thousandth of a tile.
  3. The passes, cheaper to the bit: 0.70 ms a step becomes 0.60 ms, every tile’s water the same.
  4. The water in Hazel: the same water to the bit, at 13 times the time.
  5. Letting seepage sleep: the water 2.5 times as cheap and the run 2.2 times as fast, with 24% fewer flooded tiles; Matthew chose it, and 0.0.151 has it.
  6. The run’s way by A*: within 32 tiles of the gnome and its target, every way found, in about half the time of 0.0.149’s search.
  7. The way in Hazel: the same ways, at 28 times the time; nearly all of it the search, not the handing over.
  8. The speed run, five ways: 65.1 s becomes 56.9 s.
  9. What Hazel would need: arrays, a way to read the page’s, and a 32-bit float.
  10. What next, and how this was put together.

In short. The water is slow because it moves: about 77% of the awake blocks move water every step, the seepage from the rivers toward the villages’ pumps, and in nearly all of them the most that moves is under a thousandth of a tile. Its passes are rewritten to be cheaper with every tile’s water the same to the bit (0.70 to 0.60 ms a step in the browser pane), and the run’s way is found by A* over a window round the gnome and its target, in about half the time of the old search. Together, the speed run takes 56.9 s where it took 65.1 s, on one GitHub runner. Both are also written in Hazel, Water.hz and Path.hz, off by default and switched on under ⚙️ Advanced or by ?water=hazel and ?way=hazel: the world save check holds each to the page’s JavaScript on every push, the same water to the bit and the very same ways. Hazel’s water takes 13 times the time, and its way 28 times. The reason is the one ^ticket(846) named: Hazel has no arrays, so a block of tiles crosses into Hazel as a list of records, and a search keeps its best costs in a balanced tree where JavaScript writes into an array. The one large saving was a game decision: letting seepage of under a thousandth of a tile sleep makes the run 2.2 times as fast, and leaves 24% fewer flooded tiles after 10,000 steps. Matthew chose it, and Gnome world 0.0.151 has it.

Where the water’s time goes#

The water costs what it does because it moves: of about 340 awake blocks of eight tiles by eight, about 261 move water every step. But in 310 of about 338 blocks changing in a step, the most that moves is under a thousandth of a tile. Since 0.0.131 the water sleeps in blocks: a block is stepped only while water moves in it, more than a ten-thousandth of a tile in a step, and for a second and a half after. The world has 2,912 blocks; after the first few seconds of a run, about 340 are awake, holding about 17,000 wet tiles, and 77% of those blocks are awake because water is still moving in them. It is seepage: water from the rivers working down through sand and gravel toward the villages’ pumps, which draw it off, so it never stops.

The passes, cheaper to the bit#

The water’s two passes are rewritten to do the same arithmetic in fewer steps, every tile’s water and every block’s waking the same to the bit: 0.70 ms a step becomes 0.60 ms in the browser pane, 14% less. The world’s arrays and the ground’s numbers are held in locals, and Math.min is written out as comparisons; no amount it compares can be −0 or not a number, so the answers are the same. The tests keep 0.0.149’s passes beside the new ones, and the world save check runs both from the tour’s world for 20 steps on every push, comparing every tile. A step was already about 11 ns a tile, so the rewrite cannot save much; the work is the number of tiles.

The water in Hazel#

Water.hz gives every tile the same water as the page’s JavaScript, to the bit, and takes 13 times the time: 7.7 ms a step against 0.60. It is the two passes, a block’s row at a time: Water.down takes the block’s eight tiles over the row beneath them, and Water.across takes them in the way the pass goes, with the tile either side of the block, which takes water and does not give it. The page calls it for each awake block of each row, in the same order its own passes go, and writes the answers back. The page keeps each tile’s water as a 32-bit float, rounded every time it is stored, and the order of the stores matters across a row; so Water.hz rounds each amount to 24 bits as it would be stored, by Veltkamp’s split (multiply by 229+1, then two subtractions), which gave the same as JavaScript’s Math.fround on each of 25 million amounts of the kinds the water makes.

A step makes about 5,937 calls: 1.43 µs each down and 2.51 µs across, of which 0.38 µs is handing the tiles over and back. In the speed run, with the water in Hazel, the compiled Hazel is 48% of the page’s busy time and garbage collection 2.9%; the frames still come at 60 a second, as 0.0.149’s budget gives the steps what a frame leaves, but a frame gets through 2 steps where it got through 14, and the run had not reached the gems in two minutes. That is what it is for: a version of the water to switch on, profile and measure.

Letting seepage sleep#

With the water’s waking threshold a thousandth of a tile, not a ten-thousandth, the water is 2.5 times as cheap and the speed run 2.2 times as fast, but 24% fewer open tiles are flooded after 10,000 steps. The water behaves differently, so it was Matthew’s decision: he chose a thousandth (2026-11-27, “Yes, a thousandth”), and 0.0.151 has it. At 10,000 steps of the run, 79 blocks are awake where 323 were, and the water takes 0.153 ms a step where it took 0.383. What sleeps is the slowest seepage: left alone, a block whose water moves less than a thousandth of a tile a step stops a second and a half later, until digging, rain or a river wakes it. The world holds 2.6% less water at 10,000 steps, and tunnels and caves fill more slowly: 1,170 open tiles half full or more, against 1,538. 0.0.150 had it as ?wateps=0.001, the setup measured here; in 0.0.151 it is the game’s, and ?wateps=0.0001 gives the water of 0.0.150 and before, for comparing. A river’s own test of a tile not at its level stays a ten-thousandth, and Water.hz wakes at a thousandth too, the same water to the bit.

The run’s way by A*#

The run’s way is now found by A* over a window: the gnome’s tile and its target’s, and 32 tiles more each way. That window reached every target tried, in about half the time of 0.0.149’s search. 0.0.149 searched from the gnome outward over the whole world until it reached the target, the cheapest way first. A* goes first where the cost so far plus a guess of what is left, 1,000 for each tile across and down, is least. The guess is never more than a way costs, so the way found is a cheapest one, as before. The costs are the old ones to the thousandth. A way underground goes round by the tunnels: within 16 tiles only 4 of 30 nearby gems were reached, within 32 all of them. So the page tries 32, then 128, then 0.0.149’s search over the whole world.

The way in Hazel#

Path.hz finds the very same ways as the page’s JavaScript twin, and takes 28 times the time: 17.6 ms to a nearby gem where wayJS takes 0.74 ms, and 85.3 ms to a thing far off against 2.7 ms. Both take the least estimate first and, among equal estimates, the least place, a total order, so any correct heap takes the tiles in the same order and both find the same way. They did on all 38 targets in the browser pane, and the world save check runs 20 more on every push. Path.hz has its own types, a tree of the window’s tiles, a balanced tree (AVL) of the best cost to each, and a pairing heap of the tiles still to take, defined inside its one function, as the world’s program has as many definitions as the browser lets it build.

Handing over the window costs little: for 10,000 tiles, 0.68 ms to turn the page’s list into Hazel’s and build the tree; for 40,000, 8.5 ms. The search is the cost: corner to corner across 10,000 open tiles, 87.82 ms in Hazel against 3.0 ms in JavaScript. Where JavaScript writes a tile’s best cost into an array, Hazel makes a new path of a balanced tree; where JavaScript swaps two slots of its heap, Hazel makes new heap nodes. A search of 16 to 85 ms in Hazel would make the very long steps ^ticket(905) set out to remove (with it on, the run’s slowest step was 68 ms), so wayJS finds the run’s way unless Path.hz is chosen.

The speed run, five ways#

On one GitHub runner, the speed run takes 56.9 s in 0.0.150 against 65.1 s in 0.0.149, 13% less, at 60 frames a second both; with the threshold at a thousandth it takes 25.8 s. A frame gets through 14 steps where it got through 12. With the threshold at a thousandth, the water of 0.0.151, a frame gets through 27. With the way in Hazel the run takes 58.7 s, its searches 3.2% of the steps, and its worst frame 83 ms. With the water in Hazel, a frame gets through 2 steps, and in two minutes the run had found 9/12 of its things and not yet begun on the trees.

What Hazel would need#

Both Hazel versions give the right answers and are slow for one reason: Hazel has no arrays. Three things would change that.

  • Arrays, read and written in place. This is ^ticket(846), which ^report(64) and the page’s own comment on its search already named. With them, the way’s best costs and its heap would be arrays, as wayJS’s are, and the water a pass over a row rather than a list built and taken apart for each block.
  • A way for compiled code to read the page’s arrays. The world lives in the page’s typed arrays. A Hazel function cannot take a JavaScript function or array as it is: the library has no converter for a function’s type, and a list is copied into Hazel’s cells. A Hazel search that could read the page’s tiles in place would not need the window handed over at all.
  • A 32-bit float. Water.hz rounds by Veltkamp’s split, which matched Math.fround on every amount tried but is three operations where a builtin would be one.

What next#

  1. Time 0.0.151’s speed run on the laptop with ⏱ under ⚙️ Advanced, in a video call and out of it, now that the water settles: the GitHub runner’s 25.8 s against a laptop’s.
  2. Arrays in Hazel (^ticket(846)), then Water.hz and Path.hz again over them, measured the same way.
  3. A converter for function-typed arguments in the hazeljs library, so a Hazel search can ask the page for each tile it reaches.
  4. The water in Hazel by rows, not blocks: one call per row and pass, with the waking handled in Hazel, to see how much of its time is the calls.
  5. Profile Path.hz’s search alone (its tree of best costs against its heap) in the browser pane, to see which persistent structure costs more.

How this was put together#

The water’s blocks and moves, the three ways of the passes, the threshold, the windows and Hazel’s way were measured in the Claude app’s browser pane on the laptop, with Gnome world’s test hooks (WorldTest.waterSame, waySame, steps), its rules built by hazel-jit at load from build.hazel.org’s hazeljs build (hazelgrove/hazel 0f24cea171). The numbers, as the pane gave them, are in data/pane.json. The speed run five ways is hazeljs-examples’ frames job (node/world-frames.mjs, ^report(68)), runs 46102840841 and 46103505251 (INTEL(R) XEON(R) PLATINUM 8573C), each setup in turn on one runner at 1280×800 for up to two minutes. Its logs are parsed by parse_frames.py, no artifact downloaded. The twins are checked on every push by the world save check (node/world-save-check.mjs): the water from the tour’s world, 20 steps, every tile compared; the way to the 20 nearest gems, every tile of each way compared.
