Why Hazel Is Slow: A Thousand Cuts

Cyrus wants to know why Hazel is slow. Matthew, 2026-11-08: "Cyrus wants to know why Hazel is slow, and I say: death by a thousand cuts. I'd like more substance behind that idea." This report is that substance: the costs this series has measured in Hazel, {{n_cuts}} of them from {{n_reports}} reports, side by side, each with its number, what it was measured on, whether it is fixed, and the design choice it comes from.

In short

Hazel is slow for many reasons at once, not for one. The series has measured {{n_cuts}} separate costs; many are most of the workload they were found in, and many make one another larger. Remove the biggest and the next is there: on the Witch slide, the trip of the worker's answer was 70 to 72% of a small edit; cut, the evaluator is now most of it. (Update, 11-08 evening: with the run compiled in the page, the evaluator is out of the way on the steps it answered, and the page's own work, 318 to 469 ms, is most of what is left: report 049.)

They come from four design choices, each sound on its own and each applied everywhere:

  • Values are held as terms ({{f_terms}} cuts): every value carries ids on every node, closures carry environments substituted into them, run-time type checks walk values, and probes write values out with their environments.
  • Every edit redoes whole-program work ({{f_whole}}): statics, parsing, reuse checks, the page's view of the whole program, undo's copies.
  • Values cross boundaries as terms ({{f_moving}}): to the worker and back, to JS and back, encoded, shared, decoded.
  • The interpreter pays for the editor at every step ({{f_machine}}): state merging, reuse checks, probe sampling and effects at every pass, trampolines for stack safety, and the garbage all of it makes.

Each choice buys something Hazel is for: holes, live evaluation, probes, a stepper, an editor that is never in an invalid state. The cost is that every one of them is paid on every value and every edit, including the many that need none of it.

The four families

Values held as terms ({{f_terms}} cuts)

A closed, first-order value pays for what a term can be: open, holed, a closure.

  • Ids. A fresh id is a UUID written into 16 bytes and made a JS string by hand: about three quarters of minting an id, and 46% of a list loop's time (^report(29)).
  • Run-time type checks. On the livelits branch, where a view is a tree of Html constructors, the ascription step was 88% of a click on the Polygons editor (15 to 17 s); #2661 took it under 6% (^report(17)). A list loop is quadratic: each iteration pushes the list's type over every element again, minting a fresh id for each part, so building and summing 3,000 numbers takes 4,591 ms against Fumola's 24.7 ms, 186 times (^report(29)).
  • Environments substituted in. A function Hazel returned, holding 4,096 numbers, took 5.31 ms and allocated 29.0 MB a call; with closures kept whole, 0.025 ms, about 215 times faster (^report(40)).
  • Probes. A probe on the Witch slide's last line, whose value is a few bytes, kept a module of functions written out whole in its environment: 3.8 MB of a 5.4 MB answer from the worker (^report(46), what the answer holds).

Whole-program work on every edit ({{f_whole}} cuts)

An edit to one literal redoes work proportional to the whole program, and sometimes to its depth exponentially.

  • Statics. On dev, statics of nested functions was exponential in their depth: 5.3 s at eight levels, 13 ms with #2603, open (^report(17)).
  • Re-evaluation. On Full Body and the Witch, the whole figure is one binding that depends on every slider, so every slider edit costs the incremental run 85 to 95% of a run from scratch (^report(11)); 75 to 84% of the steps it takes are in calls whose inputs did not change (^report(10)).
  • The page's view. On #2597, rebuilding the code editor's view of the whole program was 77% of the main thread while a slide loaded (^report(7)); a drag's every preview ran a whole-program reuse check, about 40% of a Full Body drag (^report(15)); on the Witch, the whole slide's view takes about 117 ms and is drawn five times an edit (^report(46)).

Values moved as terms across boundaries ({{f_moving}} cuts)

A value leaving the evaluator is encoded with all its ids, and was hashed node by node to find what the other side held. On the Witch slide, the worker's answer going to the page was 70 to 72% of a small edit: shared in 1.4 to 1.6 s, encoded in 1.1 to 1.2 s, decoded in 1.4 to 2.0 s with the page frozen (^report(46), the trip). As data instead of terms, the slide's picture is 3 times smaller and 2.5 to 3.9 times quicker to send (^report(47), experiment 1).

An interpreter that pays for the editor at every step ({{f_machine}} cuts)

Every term the evaluator visits, values included, goes through state merging, a reuse check, probe sampling and recording effects before its rule. On a loop, the rule itself is 7% of the time; garbage collection is 36%, and the stages and their trampolines 37% (^report(29)).

How the cuts multiply

The cuts are not only many; they make one another larger, so removing one can be worth more than its own share.

  1. The Witch's answer. A probe keeps every name its expression uses; substitution writes each out whole; so a module became 3.8 MB. The sharing pass then hashed every value of that answer and found almost nothing to share; encoding and decoding scaled with its size, and decoding froze the page. Cutting the module and the trace gave 2.2 to 3.2 times; dropping the sharing, 2.9 to 4.6 times in all; and the evaluator's own time fell too, unexplained (^report(46)).
  2. A list loop. A type pushed over a growing list each iteration (quadratic), a fresh id for every part it touches (each id three quarters a JS copy of 16 bytes), then the garbage of all of it: the gap to Fumola grows with the list, to 186 times at 3,000 numbers (^report(29)).
  3. Closures. A closure's environment is substituted into its body, so a value it holds is walked again on every call: 5.31 ms and 29 MB a call at 4,096 numbers (^report(40)).
  4. Nesting. Whole-program work inside per-item work becomes quadratic, and nested analysis exponential: nested functions' statics exponential in their depth, 5.3 s at eight levels (^report(17)).
  5. One binding, many sliders. Reuse works per top-level binding, and a picture is one binding over every slider, so a step of any slider re-evaluates 85 to 95% of the run, even when nothing in the picture changes (^report(11)).

Where the fixes are

Most fixes in the ledger are on the livelits branches (#2635, #2597) or on trial branches; of the PRs against dev it names, two are merged, #2602 and #2679, and nine are open: #2603, #2608, #2610, #2614, #2661, #2671, #2675, #2681 and #2689. So dev pays most of these cuts today. Dev also pays more for the worker's answer than the branches measured: it sends the run's final state as it is, the incremental cache and the trace included, and the cache comes back with the next request (^report(47)).

Beside Fumola

Hazel's evaluator takes about half the steps Fumola's machine takes, and each costs about 20 times as much: 2.6 µs a pass against 0.13 µs a step. Net, Fumola is 10 times faster on a loop and 6.5 times on calls (^report(29)). Fumola keeps its values apart from its terms, with no ids in them, and its first-order values serialize as they are (^report(47), beside Fumola's Value).

In order of what they cost to do: first merge what is already built and measured, then port what the branches proved, then change the three choices that make most of the cuts, and track the goal in CI as each lands.

2. Port what the branches measured

  • The Witch's cuts (^report(46), trial branch): a value holding a function left out of a probe sample's environment; the trace cleared before the worker's answer leaves; the answers unshared. A small edit from 4.2–6.5 s to 1.3–1.7 s. Dev has the same Sample.Env.elide, keeps the trace, and also sends its cache both ways (#2606 above).
  • Closures kept whole (^report(40), a trial): a function holding 4,096 numbers, 5.31 ms and 29.0 MB a call, to 0.025 ms and 93 KB.
  • Views compiled to JS (^report(45), the livelits line): a Polygons render, model to page, 14.1 ms on the interpreter and 0.38 ms compiled, drawn straight from JS.

3. Change the three choices behind most cuts

  • Values held as data, not terms, when closed and first-order: no source ids, no environments, no sharing (^ticket(718), ^report(47)). It reaches the {{f_terms}} cuts of the first family and the {{f_moving}} of the third. Its first experiment: the Witch's picture 3 times smaller and 2.5 to 3.9 times quicker to send.
  • Work in proportion to the edit: reuse below a top-level binding, where 75 to 84% of an incremental run's steps are in calls whose inputs did not change (^report(10)); statics per item; for a change to one livelit, no whole-page view (the Witch's is about 117 ms, five times an edit). It reaches the {{f_whole}} cuts of the second family.
  • Compiled code for the run, as for views: under ideal conditions (no holes, no probes shown, an edit to livelit models only), the run in JS on the main thread, with nothing sent (^report(46), compiling the run). It reaches the {{f_machine}} cuts of the fourth family, and it is the road to a 60 fps floor. (Update, 11-08 evening: measured in the app, report 049: the Witch's run, compiled, 10 to 12 ms a step in Firefox, its picture at the page's first redraw on two of four steps.)

4. Track the goal on dev

A small edit's time, from the edit to the picture, on a dev slide in Firefox on GitHub's machines, for every PR, against 16.7 ms. CI already fails a PR whose evaluation exceeds its step budget (#2678); steps do not show the trip, the page's view or the garbage. The Firefox check that measured the Witch (scripts/jit_slides.py --edit, on the livelits line) can run on dev's slides.
