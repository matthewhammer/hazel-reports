In short

We agree: a few well-chosen fixes, each written by hand and reviewed, are worth more than many separate ones, and our own record on the hazel-jit branch shows the risk he names. Report 048's ledger files each of its 196 costs under one of {{n_causes}} root causes. Six fixes would address the causes of {{covered}} of them: Merkle hashes in the grammar, ids made cheap and kept temporary inside the evaluator, a value's type checked once, the worker as a language server, per-item statics landed on dev, and derived state and rendering through Bonsai. All six come from his message.

Wherever the series has numbers on one of his points, they bear out his gut reaction, with two refinements: a hash belongs on the node it describes, not in a side table, and a probe's environments are large only where they cross to the page, not in memory. The code the agent wrote stays a prototype. It answered fast whether a livelit's slider can show its step in tens of milliseconds, and which of the changes mattered; report 052 ranks them and orders a redo by hand in small increments.

Matthew Keenan's message

His overall point: an agent fixing the costs it found one at a time would spread special-case logic through the codebase; a few well-chosen, well-implemented fixes and simplifications would do more in the long run. He sent it on Slack on 2026-11-09, in reply to report 048, as gut reactions rather than invariants, and asked to hear about any important facets he skipped (below). It is quoted as he wrote it.

What the prototype is for

The hazel-jit branch answered, in five days, whether a livelit's slider can show its step in the picture in tens of milliseconds. It can: 20 to 30 ms on the Witch slide, from 4.2 to 6.5 s. Its code measures what each change is worth; it is not code to land. That is the use Matthew Hammer names for agent-written code. Report 052 ranks the ten changes that mattered by how much each helped and orders a redo by hand in nine increments, each its own pull request with its own tests. Our own record there is the risk Matthew Keenan describes: 120 commits on one branch, 6 of its 10 ingredients without a test of their own, 10 commits that only fixed the one before, and caches keyed on object identity, two of which made a drag slower before they were fixed.

Ticket 721, finished this morning as build 45, shows both sides. When a slider's step changes only the literals in a livelit's model, the item it edited is patched rather than analyzed again: a step's statics went from 19 to 53 ms on build 43 to 12 to 18 ms on build 45, and the edited item's part from 13 to 42 ms to 3 to 6 (one run each, on the Witch slide, below). That answers a question: finer-grained incremental statics would buy 10 to 35 ms on a slider step. It is also special-case logic: one kind of edit, patched by hand inside DefStatics, and a first attempt (build 44) sent the worker the whole program instead of the edited item. We propose keeping it as that measurement, and not landing it ahead of per-item statics on dev (fix 5).
