Gnome World: Which Parts Are Hazel

Matthew asked, on 2026-11-24: “what parts of gnome world logic are expressed in JS vs Hazel? event handling? physics? global game world state? procedural rendering of trees, and other elements?” This report answers for Gnome world 0.0.103 (matthewhammer/hazeljs-examples 02b73ab), its rules compiled in the page by hazel-jit from the hazeljs branch’s build (#2857).

Outline

  1. The split: Hazel decides what the world is and how the gnome moves; JavaScript keeps the state, takes the input, moves everything else and draws.
  2. One step of the world: Hazel is called once for the gnome and, every sixth step, once a beaver; its share of the step’s time is under 1%.
  3. Event handling: every input is JavaScript’s; Hazel sees one record a step and answers with one.
  4. Physics: the gnome’s movement is Hazel; the water, the boulders, the animals and the rabbit’s rules around it are JavaScript.
  5. The world’s state: all of it in JavaScript; Hazel keeps nothing between calls.
  6. Procedural drawing: Hazel plans where things are and grows the roots; JavaScript draws everything.
  7. Why the line falls there: what Hazel in a page can and cannot do well today.
  8. Moving the logic into Hazel, piece by piece: the plan, eight pieces in order, each its own module calling nothing in World, each checked against the JavaScript it replaces and timed.
  9. Piece 1: the magic carpet’s flight: done in 0.0.107; the same answer at every step, and 1.5 µs a step in Hazel against 0.27 in JavaScript, a ten-thousandth of a frame.
  10. Piece 2: Clover’s ride: done in 0.0.134; two functions round the gnome’s step, the same ride at every step, 0.89 µs a step in Hazel against 0.28 in JavaScript; and each piece so far, timed the same way.
  11. Piece 3: the boulders: done in 0.0.135; a boulder’s wobble, fall, landing and hit, the same boulders and hearts at every step; 0.26 µs a boulder’s step in Hazel against 0.03 to 0.09 in JavaScript.
  12. Piece 4: picking things up: done in 0.0.136; which things and hearts the gnome takes and what each gives it, the same at every step; 2.22 µs a step in Hazel against 0.50, over half of it the world’s lists crossing.
  13. Piece 5: the wind: done in 0.0.137; its aim and gusts, five random numbers a step given in by the page, the same wind at every step; 0.37 µs a step in Hazel against 0.095.
  14. What next, and how this was put together.

Update, 2026-11-25. Since this report first looked (at 0.0.103), six pieces of Gnome world’s logic have become Hazel’s: the magic carpet’s flight (0.0.107), the cat tail’s flight (written in Hazel from the start, 0.0.116), Clover’s ride (0.0.134), the boulders (0.0.135), picking things up (0.0.136) and the wind (0.0.137), in a migration Matthew asked for, “More and more of gnome world’s game logic into hazel, from JS”, and went on with: “porting the gnome world JS into Hazel, module by module ... let’s profile the difference as we go”. This report is revised as the work goes (the plan, piece 1, piece 2, piece 3, piece 4, piece 5, each piece so far). The sections before them describe 0.0.103, as they did; since then the water has gone from 2.2 ms a step to about 0.6 (0.0.131, stepped only where it moves).

The split

Hazel decides what the world is and how the gnome moves; JavaScript does everything that touches the tiles, the screen or the clock. By size, the Hazel is 40,213 characters of code in 32 functions (World.hz, comments left out), and the JavaScript 443,696 in 160 functions (app.js): 92% of the code, 70% of it drawing.

The Hazel is a set of pure functions the page calls. Each takes small records and returns one:

One step of the world

In a step (a sixtieth of a second), the page calls Hazel once, for the gnome, and every sixth step once for each of the five beavers; everything else in the step is JavaScript. World.step takes 0.016 ms of a step of about 2.4 ms before drawing; the water alone takes 2.2 ms. Then the frame is drawn, all of it in JavaScript.

Event handling

Every input is JavaScript’s; Hazel sees one record a step and answers with one. Listeners for keys and for the pad’s buttons set S.keys. Each step the page packs the held keys into a Pad record (dx, up, down, jump, dig) and calls World.step(gnome, near, pad). The step answers with the gnome as it is now, its ev saying what happened, and the page makes the effects: the dug tile taken out of the arrays (digOut), a sound, a heart lost.

Building (B), riding (R), the map, saving, loading and restarting never pass through Hazel. Nor does the speed run’s planning: it finds its way over the tiles in JavaScript and then presses the keys, so the gnome it drives still moves by World.step.

Physics

The gnome’s movement is Hazel; every other thing that moves is JavaScript. World.step moves a box 0.66 tiles wide and 0.88 tall in thousandths of a tile: running at 92 a step, eased; falling 11 faster a step, to 267 at most; a jump of −208 cut short when up is let go; climbing at 70; floating, swimming and diving; walking on the diagonals of sloped banks and sliding down them; digging the tile pushed into. It sees the world only as the 3 by 5 tiles round the gnome, packed into five whole numbers.

JavaScript moves the rest, each step: the water seeping through the ground (every wet tile, down and then across), the boulders falling, the pebbles, the wind and its gusts, the leaves, the birds’ flights, the squirrels along the branches, the frogs’ jumps, the bunnies’ hops, the villagers walking and a felled tree falling. The beavers are both: World.beaver decides each one’s job, and JavaScript eases its body toward where Hazel put it.

Clover the rabbit is Hazel’s gnome step with JavaScript rules around it. The page changes what the step sees (the window above), what it is asked (twice as fast: dx 2) and what comes back: a hop’s or a leap’s speed set after the jump, the digging’s progress tripled, a landing in the water undone. It went in JavaScript because World.hz is full: one more top-level definition overflowed the browser’s stack building the module on 2026-11-23.

The world’s state

All of it lives in JavaScript; Hazel keeps nothing between calls. One object, S, holds the gnome, the rivers and dams, the trees, the beavers, the villagers, the animals, Clover, the weather, the camera and the speed run; nine arrays hold the 186,368 tiles (896 by 208): what is there, its water, what is behind and on it, gems, stones, slopes and roots, and the top of each column, about 2.05 MB.

So saving is JavaScript’s: a save file is the seed and a snapshot of S and the tiles, and the GitHub check hashes its 36 parts before a save and after a load. The plan is never saved: Hazel makes it again from the seed.

Procedural drawing

Hazel plans where things are and grows the roots; JavaScript draws everything, 311,422 characters of canvas code.

  • Trees. World.plan puts each tree down with its kind and height. JavaScript grows its limbs (growLimbs: forking from seeded random numbers), puts leaf pads from eight shapes at their tips, and paints the tree once into a picture, in strips eight tiles tall for the biggest.
  • Great trees. Hazel gives each limb (World.limbs: how high, which side, how long, the house on it); JavaScript grows the drawn crown to reach them.
  • Roots and mycelium. Here Hazel draws too: World.roots and World.threads grow each tree’s roots and threads as lines, as Gnome dig grows them, and JavaScript strokes the lines.
  • Everything else, the gnomes, birds, rabbits, houses, rivers and sky, is JavaScript.

Why the line falls there

Hazel takes what is a function of small records; JavaScript keeps what is big, changing or drawn. What Hazel in a page cannot do well today decides most of the rest:

  • No arrays: 186,368 tiles a step is a loop over typed arrays in JavaScript.
  • One module’s size: World.hz is at the browser’s limit for building one module (32 top-level definitions), and a module that calls another costs the build seconds.
  • The page’s edge: whole numbers past 2³¹ from JavaScript arrive cut to 32 bits; tuples do not cross, records do; every crossing converts values.
  • Speed: compiled by hazel-jit, a step is 0.016 ms; interpreted (hazeljs 0.0.4 from npm), the page’s own note says about 4 ms, too slow for 60 steps a second with drawing.

Moving the logic into Hazel, piece by piece

The plan: eight pieces, in order, each the move of one part of the step from the page’s JavaScript into a Hazel module of its own, each done when its Hazel gives the same answers as the JavaScript it replaces and is timed against it. Matthew asked on 2026-11-25: “More and more of gnome world’s game logic into hazel, from JS. Suggest a migration plan, where we work piece by piece. We have a report about this topic already. Let’s revise and amend that document as we work. Suggest the first piece to migrate, and without waiting for me, do it, and measure the performance difference pre/post migration.” The order puts first what fits Hazel as it is today (a function of small records, called each step), then what needs more of it.

Every piece works under the same rules, learned on the first:

  • A module of its own, calling nothing in World. World.hz is at the browser’s limit for one module’s definitions, and a module that calls another costs the build seconds. The new module reads the same window of tiles World.step reads, and its own helpers (a remainder, the window’s tiles) live inside its one function.
  • No new names in the preamble. The program as a whole sits at the browser’s stack for building it: two record types added to the preamble overflowed it (Stack_overflow, in the page) where the same types written out in the function’s signature did not. The least stack the program builds in, in Node, is 975 KB with the new module and without it.
  • The JavaScript stays beside it, made to agree. The JavaScript is rewritten as a pure function of the same records, giving the same answer (the page’s own while the rules build, or if an edit breaks them; and a switch in the page to use it), and a check in Node, also run on GitHub, takes the JavaScript from the page’s own file and compares the two at every step of a long random run.
  • Timed before and after, a step of each in Node and in the page, and the step’s share of a frame.

Piece 1: the magic carpet’s flight

Done in Gnome world 0.0.107: Fly.step in Hazel gives the same answer as the page’s JavaScript at every one of 20,000 steps, and takes 1.5 µs a step to the JavaScript’s 0.27, in Node; 0.009% of a frame. N’s magic carpet came in 0.0.106, its flight written in JavaScript: it floats where it is until an arrow is held, its speed comes up in a few steps and stops as quickly, fast across, through open air and not through ground, rock, a boulder or into the water. It was the first piece because it is a function of small records called each step, it reads only the window World.step reads, and it was new, so the JavaScript it replaced was known exactly.

  • Rules/Fly.hz, one function, Fly.step(gnome, near, pad, carpet), in Hazel’s editor beside World on the page: the speed eased toward the keys’ (233 thousandths of a tile a step across, 150 up or down), each axis moved if the box (0.76 tiles across, from 0.92 above the feet to 0.28 below them) meets nothing there, the gnome and the carpet given back.
  • The same answer: 20,000 of 20,000 steps in Node (the flight moved at 16,689 of them and was held back by something at 2,572), and 3,000 of 3,000 in the page in world 7. The check runs in the world save check on GitHub with each push.
  • The time: 5.6 times the JavaScript’s in Node on the laptop, 5.8 times on GitHub’s runner (0.21 µs against 1.25, the world save check of 0.0.107), 7.7 times in the page, and either way thousandths of a millisecond in a frame of 16.7. Most of Hazel’s time is the crossing: four records converted in and two out, each step, where the JavaScript reads them where they are (^report(65), Conversion at the edges).

Piece 2: Clover’s ride

Done in Gnome world 0.0.134: Ride.pre and Ride.post in Hazel give the same answers as the page’s JavaScript at all 20,000 made-up steps in Node, and the same ride, step for step, in the page; her ride takes 0.89 µs a step in Hazel against 0.28 in JavaScript in Node (3.2 times), 3.97 against 0.83 in the page (4.8 times): a few thousandths of a percent of a frame. Matthew, after the first piece and the cat tail: “I’m inspired by going further with porting the gnome world JS into Hazel, module by module. please keep going, and let’s profile the difference as we go.”

  • Rules/Ride.hz, two functions round the gnome’s own step, World.step, in Hazel’s editor beside World on the page. Ride.pre(gnome, keys, clover): the keys as the page holds them and her state (a press of up kept for when she lands, whether she leapt with up still held, in the air from a hop or a leap, her last safe place) give the keys World.step gets (across twice as fast, but not digging; up held while she rises, so a hop or a leap is not cut short), whether this step leaps, and her state. Ride.post(gnome, keys, clover, leap, wet): World.step’s gnome becomes hers: a jump taken is her leap (360 thousandths of a tile a step) or her hop (165); she digs three times as fast; in the water without the diving gear, not climbing, back to her last safe place; standing dry, a new safe place.
  • What stays in the page: her window of tiles, built from the world (where water walls her in, what she may climb: dug earth, ladders, stairs, hatches), as the gnome’s own window is, and whether the water has reached her safe place (the page holds the water; it says so to Ride.post as one Bool). Her state is the record the page saved before, so a save is unchanged.
  • The same answers. In Node (node/fly-check.mjs, also in the world save check on GitHub with each push): 20,000 of 20,000 made-up steps, the gnome on the ground or in the air, climbing or in the water, a jump just taken or not, a safe place or none, wet or dry (4,732 leaps, 2,698 hops, 934 times back to a safe place, 3,307 new safe places). In the page: the same scripted ride of 3,000 steps from the same fresh world, once with Hazel’s and once with the JavaScript (running, digging down, leaping, hopping), the gnome and her state the same at all 3,000 steps (2,806 places along the way, 42 leaps, 2 hops).
  • The time, her ride’s own part a step, pre and post together, records in and out included: in Node on the laptop, 0.892 µs in Hazel, 0.283 in JavaScript (the median of three runs, each the median of seven batches of the 20,000; hazel-lib.js of build.hazel.org, hazel-jit as of 2026-11-25); in the page, in the app’s browser pane (Chromium 152), 3.97 µs and 0.83 (the median of three rides each way, each timed call by call, the timing’s own cost in both). The ride’s step, World.step included, is 1 to 1.7 ms either way, as the world is new: the water settling, not her ride.
  • The stack: a module of its own again, its record written out in its signature; the program builds in the same least stack in Node, 968 KB, and in the page.

Piece 3: the boulders

Done in Gnome world 0.0.135: Boulder.step in Hazel gives the same answer as the page’s JavaScript for all 20,000 made-up boulders in Node, and the same boulders and hearts, step for step, in the page; a boulder’s step takes 0.26 µs in Hazel against 0.03 to 0.09 in JavaScript in Node (3 to 8 times, the JavaScript’s own time moving from run to run), 0.316 against 0.028 in the page (11.4 times). The boulder’s JavaScript is the shortest of the pieces, so against it Hazel’s cost shows the most; and it costs the least in a frame, since only a boulder that wobbles or falls is stepped.

  • Rules/Boulder.hz, one function, Boulder.step(boulder, around), in Hazel’s editor on the page. The boulder: its tile, how far it has fallen into the tile below (a part of a tile), its wobble’s steps left, whether it falls. With open air under it, a still boulder begins to wobble, three quarters of a second (45 steps); then it falls, 0.11 of a tile a step in the air, 0.04 in the water, and lands where the tile under the next is not open; falling over the gnome (its feet within 0.8 of a tile across, its body under the boulder’s), it hits it. The answer: the boulder, and whether it began to wobble, landed or hit, for the page to sound and to hurt.
  • What stays in the page: what is round each boulder (the tile under it open, no ground, no plank, no other boulder there; the tile under that; the water; the gnome’s feet), and the boulders themselves, a list the page keeps (the world’s tiles mark them too). The page skips a boulder that is still with ground under it, so a step calls Hazel only for the boulders that move.
  • The same answers. In Node (node/fly-check.mjs, also in the world save check on GitHub with each push): 20,000 of 20,000 made-up boulders, still or wobbling or falling, wet or dry, ground under them or not, the gnome near or far (2,435 wobbles begun, 154 falls begun, 348 landings, 2,237 hits). In the page: a fresh world, the ground dug out three tiles deep under twelve of its boulders and the gnome under the first, 600 steps once with Hazel’s and once with the JavaScript and once more with Hazel’s: the boulders and the hearts the same at all 600 steps each time (121 states of the boulders along the way, one hit).
  • A bug the check found. The first page check had Hazel’s run lose a heart and the JavaScript’s not, the boulders the same. It was the page, not either rule: a hit leaves the gnome unhurtable for a second and a half of the clock, and a world built within that time kept it, so the second run in a row dodged the hit, whichever went second. A new world now starts with that time past (0.0.135).
  • The time, a boulder’s step, records in and out included: in Node on the laptop 0.261 µs in Hazel, 0.084 in JavaScript (the median of three runs, each the median of seven batches of the 20,000; hazel-lib.js of build.hazel.org, hazel-jit as of 2026-11-25). The JavaScript’s step is so short, a few comparisons and sums, that in Node it came out at about 0.035 µs in two of five runs and about 0.09 in the other three, as V8 compiled it; Hazel’s at 0.24 to 0.27 every time; in the page, in the app’s browser pane (Chromium 152), the 900 calls of the check’s run replayed 200 times each way, nine batches, the median: 0.316 µs and 0.028. Hazel’s 0.26 µs is the least of the pieces, as its records are the smallest; most of it is likely the crossing (two small records in, a record holding a record out), which the JavaScript does not pay. In the page’s run about one boulder and a half moved a step (1,906 calls in 1,200 steps): half a microsecond of a 16.7 ms frame.
  • The stack: a module of its own, its records written out in its signature; the program builds in the same least stack, 968 KB in Node, and in the page.

Piece 4: picking things up

Done in Gnome world 0.0.136: Pickup.step in Hazel gives the same answers as the page’s JavaScript at all 20,000 made-up steps close by and all 20,000 as in a world, in Node, and the same pickups, step for step, in the page; a step takes 2.22 µs in Hazel against 0.50 in JavaScript in Node (4.5 times), 1.42 against 0.15 in the page (9.5 times). It is the first piece that takes lists, and the lists are over half its cost: in the page about 0.54 µs a call with both lists empty, and about 18 ns more for each thing or heart in them.

  • Rules/Pickup.hz, one function, Pickup.step(me, things, hearts), in Hazel’s editor on the page. The gnome (its feet in tiles, its hearts, what it has: the diving gear, its tool, its kit of shoes, flippers and climbing claws as one number, the climbing pack, its carrots), the world’s things (where each lies, what it is, whether it is taken) and its hearts. A thing is taken when the gnome’s middle, half a tile above its feet, comes within 0.8 of a tile of it across and 1 up or down, in the page’s order; it gives the climbing pack, the diving gear, a carrot, the shoes, flippers or claws, or a tool (the better kept). A heart, the same, only while the gnome has fewer than five, and no more than make five. The answer: the gnome with what it took, and which things and which hearts it took, by their places in the page’s lists.
  • What stays in the page: the lists themselves (the page draws them, the speed run steers by them, a save keeps them), marking each taken, the sounds, the notes (the game paused while one is read), what only the page holds (the carpet and the cat tail as rides, the gem finder’s compass), a heart’s coming back once its place is out of view, and a gem lying loose in an open tile, a read of one tile.
  • The same answers. In Node (node/fly-check.mjs, also in the world save check on GitHub with each push): 20,000 of 20,000 made-up steps close by, one to six things and up to four hearts within a few tiles of the gnome, the gnome with any hearts and anything (14,787 things and 6,706 hearts taken, at 13,098 steps); and 20,000 of 20,000 as in a world, the page’s twelve things and twenty-six hearts over 900 tiles (something taken at 10). In the page: a fresh world, two hearts left, the gnome put by each of its twelve things in turn and by six of its hearts, three steps each, then 120 steps more; once with Hazel’s, once with the JavaScript, once more with Hazel’s: the gnome, what it has, its hearts and every thing’s and heart’s state the same at all 174 steps (all twelve things taken, each giving what it gives; three hearts, to five, and no more).
  • The time, a step of picking up with the world’s lists: in Node on the laptop 2.22 µs in Hazel, 0.50 in JavaScript (the median of three runs, each the median of seven batches of the 20,000; hazel-lib.js of build.hazel.org, hazel-jit as of 2026-11-25); in the page, in the app’s browser pane (Chromium 152), the world’s lists with the gnome at 900 places, 50 times each, nine batches, the median: 1.418 µs and 0.149. Taken apart, in the page: both lists empty, 0.54 µs in Hazel and 0.04 in JavaScript; the twelve things only, 0.78; the twenty-six hearts only, 1.10; both, 1.23. So each thing or heart costs Hazel about 18 ns a step, converted and walked, against JavaScript’s 3. Under ⏱ the part is 1.7 to 3.8 µs a step with Hazel, 0.33 with the JavaScript (the loose gem’s tile and the timing’s own cost in both): about a hundredth of a percent of a 16.7 ms frame.
  • What it says for the next pieces: a list crosses whole, each step, whether or not anything in it is near. The page could hand over only the things within a few tiles (the plan’s “the places near it”), which would make the cost the call’s alone; at 0.7 µs a step to save, it was not worth a second rule for nearness in the page. The animals’ minds (piece 6) will take lists too, and there it may be.
  • The stack: a module of its own, its records written out in its signature and in its helpers’; the program builds in the same least stack, 968 KB in Node, and in the page.

Piece 5: the wind

Done in Gnome world 0.0.137: Wind.step in Hazel gives the same answer as the page’s JavaScript at all 60,000 steps of a wind blowing in Node, and the same wind, step for step, in the page; a step takes 0.37 µs in Hazel against 0.095 in JavaScript in Node (3.9 times), 0.321 against 0.081 in the page (4.0 times). It is the first piece whose randomness comes in from the page, and the change that asked for is one to the game, not to its speed.

  • Rules/Wind.hz, one function, Wind.step(wind, frame, r), in Hazel’s editor on the page. The wind: how strong it blows now, the strength it is turning toward (its aim), the step at which it picks a new aim, and a gust on top. A new aim every 6 to 16 seconds, now and then a strong one; the wind comes round to its aim slowly, 0.4% of the way a step; a gust comes now and then, more often the stronger the wind, its way the aim’s, and dies away, 2.5% a step. The answer: the wind, and how strong it blows this step (the wind and its gust, −1.4 to 1.4), which the leaves, the trees, the grass, the waves, the smoke, the rain, the clouds and the birds read.
  • Random numbers given in. The page’s JavaScript drew from the world’s random stream as it went: three numbers when a new aim came, one each step for whether a gust came, one more for its size. A Hazel function has no stream to draw from, so the page draws five each step, whatever the wind uses, and gives them in (r: whether a new aim is strong, the aim, how long until the next, whether a gust comes, its size). The same draws go to Hazel’s and to the JavaScript, so either way a world blows the same; but the stream now moves five a step where it moved one or two, so for the same seed the weather, and every random thing after it in the step, goes differently from 0.0.136’s. The world’s plan, from Hazel and the seed, does not change.
  • What stays in the page: the random stream, the leaves a strong wind blows off the trees (they read the wind; the trees are the page’s), and everything that sways or drifts with it.
  • The same answers. In Node (node/fly-check.mjs, also in the world save check on GitHub with each push): a wind blowing from the page’s first wind for 60,000 steps, five made-up random numbers a step, each step’s answer compared and the JavaScript’s carried on: 60,000 of 60,000 (97 new aims, 300 gusts, at its strongest 1.40). In the page: a fresh world, 3,000 steps, once with Hazel’s, once with the JavaScript, once more with Hazel’s, the wind, its strength, the leaves and the gnome the same at all 3,000 steps each time (4 new aims, 18 gusts).
  • A finding along the way: the page’s randomness follows its clock. The page check first parted at about step 1,100. It was not the wind: two runs both with Hazel’s wind parted too, at step 852, and two both with the JavaScript at the same step. Something in the page’s step draws from the random stream by the real clock (how many draws, or whether one is made, depends on performance.now()), so two runs from one world agree only while their timings do. One place the step reads the clock is the frogs on the lily pads, whose height is the waves’ at the clock’s time (the page’s wildStep); whether that is what moves the stream is still to be found. With the page’s clock tied to its steps, as the speed-run check already ties it (^report(65)), the runs agree at every step; the comparison above is so. The pieces before this one did not show it: they compared what does not read the stream.
  • The time, a step of the wind: in Node on the laptop 0.37 µs in Hazel, 0.095 in JavaScript (the median of three runs, each the median of seven batches of the 60,000; hazel-lib.js of build.hazel.org, hazel-jit as of 2026-11-25); in the page, in the app’s browser pane (Chromium 152), 3,000 steps of a wind 30 times each way, nine batches, the median: 0.321 µs and 0.081. One call a step, small records: a few thousandths of a percent of a 16.7 ms frame.
  • The stack: a module of its own, its records written out in its signature; the program builds in the same least stack, 968 KB in Node, and in the page.

How this was put together

Read World.hz, preamble.hz and app.js at matthewhammer/hazeljs-examples 02b73ab (0.0.103). Sizes: each Hazel top-level definition’s code with its comments taken out; the JavaScript’s top-level functions grouped by name (drawing: draw*, paint*, sprites, the gnome, birds, rabbits, trees, the HUD; simulation: tick and the steps it calls, the ride; building the world: build and its helpers; the speed run: bot*; save and load; the rest). Times: in the page with ?test=1, on the laptop, in the app’s browser pane (Chromium), the rules compiled by hazel-jit from build.hazel.org’s hazeljs build; each Hazel function called the stated number of times and averaged, once (one run is not a trend). The step’s 2.4 ms and the water’s 2.2 ms come from timing added for a while during 0.0.102’s development. The roots are World.roots’ and World.threads’ own output for the first oak of world 7. The migration’s first piece (2026-11-25): matthewhammer/hazeljs-examples 0.0.107 (11ae1ac), node/fly-check.mjs in Node 22 on the laptop with the library of hazelgrove/hazel hazeljs at 2c4a61560f (data/fly-check.json), the least stack it builds in found by halving (data/stack-margin.txt), and the page’s numbers in the browser pane with ?test=1.
